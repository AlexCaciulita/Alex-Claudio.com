02 / The collection

Six, eight,
or ten hours?

Choose a coverage window that holds the moments you care about, from the first cup of coffee to the last dance you want remembered.

Alex Claudio Photography / Planning notes

Start with two moments: the first one you want photographed and the last one you would be disappointed to miss.

Perhaps they are getting dressed and the first dance. Perhaps they are the ceremony and the final song. The time between them is a more useful starting point than your guest count or a package described as "full day."

Our wedding collections include six, eight, or ten consecutive hours, with both of us photographing throughout the booked coverage. Here is how we suggest choosing between them.

01

Put your priorities on the schedule first

Each of you should name a few things you especially want to remember. Include people as well as events: a quiet moment with a parent, friends getting ready together, or relatives who are rarely in the same room.

Mark those moments on your draft schedule. Add time for us to arrive and settle in, then leave enough room at the end that a late dinner or a long hug does not push your final priority outside coverage.

For example, if a private letter exchange at 1 p.m. and a dance at 10 p.m. both matter to you, they are already nine hours apart. Eight hours cannot cover both. You could extend coverage, move something if it suits the day, or decide which part you want photographed.

Make that choice before you book, while the schedule is still flexible.

02

Six hours: choose a focused part of the day

Six hours can suit a celebration whose main events happen close together, particularly when preparations, ceremony, and reception require little movement between locations.

An illustrative 2-8 p.m. window might include final preparations, a 4 p.m. ceremony, portraits, dinner, and early reception events. That only works if those events are genuinely scheduled within the window, with enough time for the portraits you want.

You may choose to leave the earlier morning or the later dancing unphotographed. That is a reasonable choice when those parts matter less to you than the ceremony and time with family.

A small wedding can still have a long schedule. A short guest list does not make a late reception finish earlier or remove travel between locations.

03

Eight hours: check the start and finish carefully

Eight hours gives more room to include preparation photographs and reception coverage in the same booking. It still needs a defined beginning and end.

With a 1-9 p.m. window, ask whether you will be getting dressed after 1 p.m., and whether the reception events you care about will finish comfortably before 9 p.m. If the first dance is scheduled at 8:55 p.m., there is almost no room for dinner or speeches to run late.

Eight hours may fit well without covering the last song. You can keep celebrating after the cameras leave. The only awkward version is when nobody knows where the coverage ends.

04

Ten hours: allow for a longer sequence

Consider ten hours when your priorities stretch from earlier preparations into later reception events, or when several locations, outfit changes, or a longer ceremony need room in the schedule.

A noon-10 p.m. booking covers a different day from a 2 p.m.-midnight booking. Both are ten hours. Choose the window around what is happening, and confirm the agreed start and finish in your proposal.

Even ten hours may not cover every part of an extended celebration. A welcome dinner on another date or an after-party outside the wedding coverage needs its own discussion.

A note to keep

Start with the first and last moments you want photographed.

05

Two photographers cover different perspectives, not extra hours

When both of us photograph an eight-hour wedding, the booking spans eight hours on the clock. It does not become sixteen hours of coverage.

Two photographers allow us to work in different places when the schedule and access permit. One might photograph each person's final preparations. During portraits, one of us may be able to photograph guests elsewhere.

We still need time to move, prepare, and gather people. We also cannot photograph you at cocktail hour while you are taking portraits somewhere else. A second photographer helps document simultaneous events; it does not remove your own scheduling choices.

Our hours are consecutive, so travel and gaps inside the booked window remain part of that time. When comparing other proposals, ask how each photographer handles those details rather than assuming the terms are identical.

07

Bring a rough plan to the conversation

You do not need a finished timeline to inquire. Send your date, venue or venues, ceremony time if known, and the first and last moments you want photographed. Mention travel, cultural or religious events, and any parts of the day whose timing cannot move.

Share your plans with us, and we can map the coverage around them before you choose a collection.
