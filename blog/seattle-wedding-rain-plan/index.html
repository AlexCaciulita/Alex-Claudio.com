01 / The setting

A Seattle wedding
rain plan.

Make a wet-weather plan you can trust, then get back to the parts of the day you are looking forward to.

Alex Claudio Photography / Planning notes

Before you spend time choosing umbrellas, choose where you would be happy to get married if it rains.

That choice clears up the practical questions: where portraits happen, how guests move, and who makes the call. You do not have to rebuild the day every time the forecast changes.

In Seattle, plan for a day with no pause in the rain. Outdoor photographs can still be available, but they should be a bonus rather than the hinge your schedule swings on.

01

Look at the backup space before you book

Ask to see the indoor ceremony arrangement during your venue visit. A room that looks generous when empty may feel different once it contains chairs, an aisle, musicians, and your floral arrangements.

Stand where you would exchange vows. Look toward the windows and toward the guests. Ask whether the room will be available at the time you need it, or whether another part of the wedding has to finish there first.

These questions are worth taking to the venue:

  • Can the indoor layout accommodate our full guest list comfortably?
  • Which parts of the outdoor design can move inside, and what would change?
  • Is the backup space included, or are there additional rental and setup costs?
  • Does the same room need to change from ceremony to dinner? Where will guests go during that work?
  • Who decides when to move inside, and when is the final deadline?
  • Is the route between spaces suitable for guests who need step-free access?

Ask your photographer to review the backup too. A smaller room may offer a good setting for portraits but be unsuitable for a large family group. Those are separate needs.

02

Give portraits their own indoor plan

An indoor ceremony does not automatically solve the photography logistics.

Choose an agreed portrait space for the two of you and a second option, if needed, for family photographs. Check access, background, room for the group, and whether staff will be setting up there during the portrait window.

A covered entrance can be useful, but find out whether guests, deliveries, or vehicles will need it at the same time. A hotel lobby may be open to visitors without being available for a planned photography session. Get permission rather than relying on how the space looked during a quiet visit.

The same care applies to public locations. Seattle Parks lists permit requirements for hired photography and separate commercial-use requirements. Check the proposed location and intended use with the relevant office before putting it on your schedule. Seattle Parks photography permits.

For the wedding day itself, an approved space at your venue is often the simpler choice than adding a separate rainy-day journey.

03

Separate the venue decision from the portrait decision

Your venue may need an early decision to arrange seating, staffing, or rentals. A short portrait session may allow more flexibility. Write down both decisions so nobody assumes the ceremony location can change at the last minute.

For example, your plan could say: the ceremony moves indoors according to the venue's agreed deadline; outdoor portraits are optional and will be reassessed with the photographer when that part of the day arrives.

Name the person who will communicate the decision. Your planner or venue coordinator should know whom to update, including the florist, musicians, transport team, and photography team. You should not have to relay the same message while getting ready.

04

Read more than the rain percentage

The National Weather Service explains that a precipitation percentage describes the chance of measurable precipitation at a particular point during a specified period. A 40% chance does not mean it will rain for 40% of the day. NWS precipitation probability guide.

As your date approaches, look at the forecast for the venue and the relevant hours, including timing, expected rainfall, and wind. Discuss it with your team at the agreed check-in, while respecting any earlier venue deadlines.

Tell your photographer what feels comfortable to you before the day. You might enjoy a brief walk in light rain, or prefer to keep all portraits indoors. The plan should allow for that preference.

05

Pack for the journey between photographs

Consider what happens between the suite, ceremony, portrait location, and reception. Those short walks still count.

Put practical items in one accessible bag: shoes suitable for the route, a towel, an extra layer, and whatever you need for a small hair or makeup touch-up. If you choose umbrellas, confirm who brings them, how many are available, and where they will be kept.

Tell your team about anything that should stay dry or cannot easily be cleaned. Arrange help with a train or veil if you are wearing one. Check that guests have somewhere to leave wet coats and umbrellas without blocking an entrance.

Allow time to get settled again after an outdoor portrait session. Going straight from a wet walkway into your ceremony with no pause is an avoidable pressure.

06

Put the final plan on one page

Before the wedding, share a short note containing:

  • The ceremony weather decision, deadline, and person responsible.
  • Confirmed indoor portrait locations and any access restrictions.
  • Guest routes, transport arrangements, and accessibility needs.
  • Who brings umbrellas and other practical supplies.
  • Whether you would like outdoor portraits if conditions allow.

Once those details are agreed, hand the weather logistics to the people helping you run the day. You should be getting ready, not checking three radar apps from the makeup chair.
