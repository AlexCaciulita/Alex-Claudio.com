03 / The timing

The photographs.
And your guests.

Build a wedding-day rhythm that gives the photographs room without asking your guests to wait for them.

Alex Claudio Photography / Planning notes

Decide how much of the reception you want to spend with your guests before you fill the afternoon with portraits.

If cocktail hour matters to you, set aside time to attend it. If seeing each other at the ceremony matters more, leave room for photographs together afterward. Both choices are valid, but they produce different schedules.

Your photographer can help plan the pictures. Your planner or coordinator needs to fit that plan alongside the ceremony, transport, catering, and everyone else's work. Ask for one agreed schedule so vendors are not working from different versions.

01

Start with the times that cannot move

Write down venue access, ceremony time and expected length, meal service, transport departures, and the end of photography coverage. Add any religious or cultural events, outfit changes, or family commitments that need their own space.

Next, check daylight for your actual date and location. The U.S. Naval Observatory provides sunset and civil-twilight times; use the correct location and daylight-saving setting. USNO sun and twilight times. It is a five-minute check that can save a surprisingly frantic portrait shuffle later.

Treat that as a planning reference. Ask your photographer to assess the venue itself, including trees, nearby buildings, indoor light, and the forecast. A published sunset time is not a promise of direct sunlight where your portraits will happen.

Do this before committing to outdoor portraits after the ceremony. A sample schedule from another season may not work for your date.

02

Choose when you want to see each other

A first look means seeing each other before the ceremony. It can let you take some or all couple, wedding-party, and family portraits earlier, depending on who is ready and available.

That also means an earlier finish for preparations. Check with the people helping you get ready before placing a first look on the schedule. Everyone needed for group photographs must know their own arrival time.

If you prefer to see each other at the ceremony, say so. Individual portraits and some separate family photographs may still happen beforehand. Reserve time afterward for photographs that need both of you, and decide how much of cocktail hour you are comfortable missing.

You do not need to choose a first look to suit a template.

03

An example with a 4 p.m. ceremony

This is an illustrative eight-hour photography schedule, from 1-9 p.m. It assumes one venue, a first look, a small agreed family list, a 30-minute ceremony, and suitable afternoon light. There is no off-site travel. Hair and makeup, if applicable, are scheduled to finish in time for dressing and portraits.

Use the sequence to start a conversation; the durations need adjusting to your people, venue, and traditions.

Time Plan
1:00-1:45 p.m. Final preparations, getting dressed, and selected personal details.
1:45-2:00 p.m. Readiness check and move to the first-look location.
2:00-2:35 p.m. First look and portraits together.
2:35-3:00 p.m. Wedding-party portraits, if you have a wedding party.
3:00-3:25 p.m. Family portraits from the agreed list.
3:25-4:00 p.m. A break for you; ceremony preparations and guest arrivals for the photography team.
4:00-4:30 p.m. Ceremony.
4:30-5:20 p.m. A brief private pause, then time with guests at cocktail hour.
5:20-5:45 p.m. Guests seated, entrance, and welcome.
5:45-7:00 p.m. Dinner; coordinate vendor meals and any planned events with catering.
7:00-7:30 p.m. Toasts or other planned reception events.
7:30-8:00 p.m. First dance and any other planned dances.
8:00-9:00 p.m. Open dancing and candid reception photographs; coverage ends at 9 p.m.

This example leaves most of cocktail hour available because the planned group portraits are already complete. It does not guarantee a photographed late-night exit, and it does not assign a fixed "sunset portrait" time. If you want an evening portrait break, find the right light window and agree the timing with your planner and catering team.

Without a first look, the post-ceremony portrait block needs rebuilding. Decide which family groups can happen earlier, estimate the remaining photographs together, and allow time before dinner. Moving all the pre-ceremony portraits into cocktail hour without checking their combined duration will not work.

04

Make the family list easy to follow

List the actual people in each photograph. "Both of us with our parents" is clearer when it includes the names of everyone who should be there, particularly when family relationships are complex.

Tell your photographer privately about sensitivities, mobility needs, or combinations you would prefer to avoid. Nobody should have to explain a difficult family situation in front of a waiting group.

Ask a person who knows the family to help gather the next group. Give participants a time and a specific meeting place before the wedding. Have your photographer estimate the portrait block from the finished list; the 25 minutes in our example is a placeholder, not a promise for every family.

A note to keep

Decide how much of the reception you want to spend with your guests.

05

Count the time between events

Getting from a suite to a courtyard includes collecting belongings, finding the lift, walking, and making sure everyone arrives. A map's driving estimate does not include boarding a shuttle, parking, or walking into the next venue.

Add those transitions explicitly. Keep time for food, water, the bathroom, and getting settled before the ceremony. When something runs late, decide with your team which optional photographs can move or be shortened. Protecting every break will serve you better than squeezing in one more picture.

Before the final schedule goes out, check that the ceremony length, family list, transport, meals, and photography finish time all match. Share the same version with everyone who needs it.
